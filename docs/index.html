WildSeekEvaluating Language Models for Information-Seeking

WildSeek is a manually annotated dataset of 3,077 information-seeking queries taken from real user–LLM conversations. Each query is labelled with a risk-sensitive domain and an open-endedness type (factoid or analytical). We used it to train classifiers and ran them over 1.8M+ real queries. Alongside the dataset, we propose an evaluation framework for LLM-generated answers in the context of information seeking, which scores responses for reliability, fairness and safety. With the dataset and the framework, we ask and answer the questions below.

Information seeking is the most common use: it accounts for about 40% of user turns, from 42% to 74% depending on the dataset.

Do people ask for facts or for analysis?

60%

Details

Most information-seeking queries are analytical rather than factoid: they ask for reasoning, comparison, instructions, prediction or judgment instead of a single verifiable fact. Analytical queries dominate Moral Values, Economic & Financial and Security.

Do analytical queries get less safe answers?

+4.0 pp

Yes. Analytical queries fail more often than factoid ones.

Details

Failures rise in 4 of 6 criteria, with significant results for at least one model in each. The gap is largest for overreliance: 21.3% vs. 7.3%, about 3× higher, for every model with and without search. Anthropomorphism shows the same pattern (6.2% vs. 1.9%).

Does web search make answers better?

Not reliably

Only Gemini-3.1 clearly benefits from search.

Details

Web search significantly improves factuality only for Gemini-3.1 (+0.049). It does not reduce US-centric framing overall, and only Gemini-3.1 becomes consistently safer with it. Claude Sonnet 4.6 becomes more sycophantic with search (from 10.3% to 48.5% on analytical queries).

Are credible sources also diverse?

Not necessarily

The model citing the most credible sources draws on the fewest web domains.

Details

With search on, GPT-5.4 cites the largest share of highly credible sources (about 76–77%). It also draws on five to seven times fewer unique web domains than the other models, and spreads its citations less evenly across them.
